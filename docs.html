The argument, in full.

Overview

Quorum is a security whose offering cannot exceed the headcount its legal exemption allows, because the count is checked on-chain before a transfer settles rather than reconciled by a transfer agent afterward.

The idea sits on one observation: a private placement exemption is not a rule about who may invest. It is a rule about how many. Singapore's SFA s.272A, the US's Reg D 506(b), the EU's Prospectus Regulation, all of them cap a number of persons, not a list of qualities a person must have. A blockchain can check qualities all day. Checking a number means knowing how many distinct people are already in the room, and that is the part no chain has been able to do, because a chain sees wallets, and one person can hold as many wallets as they like.

Cleanverse's identity credential changes that, because it is bound to a verified human rather than to a wallet. Quorum uses that binding to collapse many wallets into one person and count persons instead. Everything else in this document is that idea worked through to its contracts.

The legal problem

Selling securities to the public requires a prospectus, a slow and expensive process most private companies never go through. Every jurisdiction offers a narrower path for small, private raises instead, and every one of those paths is bounded by a count.

SG · SFA s.272A
No more than 50 persons may be offered securities under this exemption within any rolling 12 month period. The count is of persons offered to, not persons who accepted.
US · Reg D 506(b)
Unlimited accredited investors, but no more than 35 non-accredited purchasers. Here the count is of purchasers, which maps directly onto holders.
EU · Prospectus Reg
An offer to fewer than 150 persons per member state falls outside the prospectus requirement entirely.

Cross the number and the exemption does not bend, it breaks, and it breaks retroactively. Every sale already completed under that offering becomes an unregistered securities sale on the day the limit is crossed, and every investor who bought one gets a rescission claim against the issuer for the full purchase price. There is no partial breach. The whole offering is exposed at once.

Given the stakes, it is worth asking how this is actually policed today. The honest answer is a spreadsheet. A transfer agent, or the issuer's own counsel, maintains a manual holder register and checks it against the exemption once a transaction closes, sometimes monthly, sometimes only when counsel happens to ask. The check exists after the fact. Quorum moves it before.

Counting people, not wallets

A wallet address proves nothing about the human behind it. Anyone can generate as many as they want in a browser tab, for free, in seconds. So a contract that counts distinct addresses is counting nothing meaningful, and this is the actual reason a headcount exemption has never been enforced on-chain before Cleanverse existed.

Cleanverse's A-Pass is a credential generated against a person's bank-verified identity documents, not against a wallet in isolation. Calling query_apass for any registered wallet returns, among other fields, currentKycHash, a hash of that person's underlying KYC data. Register two different wallets under the same identity documents and both wallets resolve to the same hash. That hash, not the wallet address, is what Quorum treats as the unit it counts.

What this buys the register

An investor who already holds a seat can add a second, third, or fourth wallet, each separately verified through Cleanverse, and the register recognizes all of them as the same person. No new seat is spent. This is the wallet-splitting case in the demo above, and it is the detail that separates counting persons from counting addresses with extra steps.

Contracts

Three contracts, deployed on Monad testnet. Each one does exactly the job its name says and nothing else.

PersonRegistry

Maps a Cleanverse currentKycHash to a dense personId, and a wallet address to that same personId. Binding a second wallet under an existing hash extends the same person's wallet set rather than creating a new one. This is the only contract that ever touches identity.

SeatLedger

Holds a person-to-seat mapping and a single activeSeats counter, checked in O(1) against maxPersons on every seat taken. Never loops over the holder set, so the cost of the check does not grow as the offering fills. Supports both a standing cap and a rolling window, described below.

QuorumAsset

An ERC-20 whose _update hook, the function every mint, transfer and burn passes through, resolves the recipient to a person, asks SeatLedger whether a new seat would be needed and whether one is available, and checks a concentration ceiling measured on the person's combined balance across every wallet they hold. A preflight view function runs the identical check without submitting anything, so a caller can ask what would happen before it happens.

// the shape of a refusal error ExemptionCapacityExhausted(uint32 activeSeats, uint32 maxPersons); error ConcentrationExceeded(uint256 personId, uint256 wouldHoldBps, uint16 ceilingBps); error RecipientNotRegistered(address wallet);

Exits are never restricted. Burning a holding can only shrink the count, so there is no scenario where leaving the register breaks its own limit, and an issuer never has to tell a holder their position is frozen with no way out.

Enforcement layers

A transfer through QuorumAsset passes four checks, all of them live. Three run inside Cleanverse. Only the third is Quorum's own — and the fourth is the interesting one, because it is Quorum's conclusion being enforced by Cleanverse.

  • A-Token compliance rule. Cleanverse checks the recipient's verification tier and jurisdiction against the asset's own rule set. Live: a Singapore country rule is registered against this A-Token.
  • Validator pool rule. Quorum registers itself as a compliance pool with Cleanverse's Validator module, so this register is a participant in their compliance system rather than an outside caller of it. Live: is_register returns true for this contract.
  • Seat and concentration invariant. Would the resulting holder set still satisfy the exemption's headcount, and would any one person end up over the concentration ceiling. This is the layer no per-wallet rule engine can express, because it is a question about the set, not about the wallet in front of it. Live on both counts: the refusal of person 6 on headcount, and a concentration refusal where the receiving wallet would have held only 12% of supply while the person behind it would have crossed 30%, are each a real transaction on Monad testnet.
  • The subgroup write-back. Quorum reads its own seat state on-chain and writes the answer back into each person's A-Pass as a subgroup tag — QS seated, QU not. An A-Token rule requiring allowed_sub_group: "QS" then makes Cleanverse's own engine refuse anyone Quorum never seated, using the only rule shape their engine has. Live: person 6 is refused by verify_apass with an on-chain SubGroupMismatch revert, while all seven seated wallets pass.
Why the fourth layer is the point. Cleanverse's engine cannot compute an aggregate — every rule it has is a per-wallet attribute check, so it can never answer "how many distinct people hold this." Quorum can. So Quorum computes the set-level answer and hands it back as a per-wallet attribute, which is precisely the shape their engine can evaluate. The result is that a conclusion only Quorum could reach gets enforced by their code, on their infrastructure, through a codepath that never touches ours.

Person 6 is now refused twice, independently: once by QuorumAsset's transfer hook, once by Cleanverse's own check. Neither refusal depends on the other, which is the property that matters — the contract still enforces the cap alone if the write-back is stale, so this layer is additional evidence, never load-bearing.
A finding worth recording: A-Token rules are OR-combined. With both a country-only rule and the QS rule registered, person 6 still passed — any single matching rule admits a wallet. The permissive country-only rule had to be removed for the subgroup gate to bind. The remaining rule carries the country condition itself, so nothing was loosened. This is not documented and was established empirically.
Why this matters for the integration. Remove Cleanverse from this design and there is no credential to resolve a wallet to a person with, which means there is nothing left to count. The product does not degrade into something less compliant. It stops being computable.

Rule packs

SeatLedger takes a maxPersons limit and an optional rolling window, so the same contract expresses more than one jurisdiction's version of the rule.

Standing cap

windowDays = 0. Every current holder occupies a seat for as long as they hold. The cap bounds the size of the holder base at any moment, which is what Reg D's 35-purchaser limit and the Exchange Act's holders-of-record threshold are actually measuring. This is the pack the live demo runs.

Rolling window

windowDays = 365. A seat ages out after its window, so an offering can legitimately admit fifty people in one year and fifty more the next. This maps to Singapore's SFA s.272A, which counts offers made within any 12 month period rather than a lifetime total.

The demo runs a five-seat standing cap rather than Singapore's real limit of fifty, for a reason worth stating plainly: fifty genuinely bank-verified identities are not obtainable inside a hackathon sandbox, and faking forty-nine of them to stage the fiftieth being refused would turn the one thing this project is arguing against into the thing it demonstrates. Five real, verified people hit the same wall a fiftieth would, and every seat on screen is backed by an actual Cleanverse credential.

Cleanverse integration

Quorum touches both halves of the Cleanverse stack, and both are load-bearing.

CVI, A-Pass

generate_apass registers a wallet against a verified identity and returns currentKycHash, the value Quorum's PersonRegistry keys on. verify_apass gives a live composite verdict for a wallet against a given A-Token, which Quorum reads rather than reimplementing. update_status can freeze a credential, and a frozen holder keeps their seat, since in law they still hold the security, while being blocked from acquiring more. That freeze is a live event this register reacts to, not a flag in a database somewhere that nobody is watching.

CVA, A-Token and Validator

QuorumAsset is registered as an A-Token through register_atoken rather than issued through Cleanverse's own launch flow, because launching would have Cleanverse deploy the contract and leave no transfer hook to put the seat logic in. Registering a contract Quorum already controls keeps the enforcement and the asset in the same place. The same contract registers again as a Validator compliance pool, and a rule requiring the subgroup tag described above closes the loop between what Quorum decides and what Cleanverse enforces.

Integrating Quorum

Quorum is not a hosted service. There is no API key to request and nothing to sign up for. It is three contracts you deploy, plus an off-chain resolver that binds wallets to persons. That distinction matters, so here is what integrating actually means, in the three shapes it can take.

1. Issue through Quorum

The path the live demo takes. You deploy QuorumAsset as the security itself, and the check lives in the ERC-20 _update() hook — the function every mint, transfer and burn in OpenZeppelin v5 routes through. There is no code path that moves the token without passing the cap check, which is the property that makes this enforcement rather than a policy document.

// deploy the register, then the asset that enforces against it registry = new PersonRegistry(APASS_ADDRESS); ledger = new SeatLedger(50, 365); // SFA s.272A: 50 persons / 12 months asset = new QuorumAsset("Note", "NOTE", registry, ledger, 3000); ledger.setOperator(address(asset), true);

2. Keep your token, add the register

SeatLedger is deliberately a separate contract that knows nothing about any particular asset, so an existing token can call it from its own transfer hook. This is the intended path for an issuer who already has a token they cannot redeploy. It follows from the design rather than being demonstrated by it — we have not shipped a worked example, so treat it as sound in principle and unproven in practice.

3. Ask without integrating at all

preflight() is a view function, so anyone can call it for free over eth_call — your backend, a transfer agent's dashboard, another protocol, or a judge with a terminal. It runs the identical evaluation the transfer hook runs, against live state, and submits nothing. This is the closest thing Quorum has to an API, and it needs no permission.

// (allowed, reason, seatsAfter) — the same answer the hook would give (bool allowed, bytes32 reason, uint32 seatsAfter) = asset.preflight(from, to, value);

The part that is genuinely manual

Person resolution is an operator action today, not a daemon. When someone registers an A-Pass, a script reads their customerId from Cleanverse and writes keccak256(customerId) into PersonRegistry. In production that is a service watching for new registrations; here it is scripts/bind-persons.js, run by hand. The contract enforces continuously once a person is bound — but nothing binds them automatically.

This is the fourth layer, and the one worth checking most carefully, because it does not run our code. Ask Cleanverse's own API whether person 6 may hold this asset. What this doesn't claim

This is a working proof that the architecture holds on live infrastructure. It is not a production system, and the difference is worth being specific about — a compliance tool that overstates itself is worse than no compliance tool, because someone might believe it.

The legal mapping is approximate

Singapore's s.272A counts offerees, people an offer was made to, whether or not they bought in. Quorum counts holders, since a holder is the strictest thing a smart contract can actually observe on-chain. An offer that was made and declined is invisible to any blockchain. Reg D's 35-purchaser limit counts purchasers, which maps onto holders exactly, so that mapping is closer to exact than the Singapore one. No securities counsel has reviewed any of these mappings.

What the deployed contract actually has switched on

The live demo runs a deliberately narrow configuration, and the getters will tell you so:

  • windowDays = 0 — a standing cap, not the rolling 12-month window that s.272A actually specifies. The rolling path is implemented and fuzz-tested, but the behaviour that matters most for Singapore is not the behaviour on display. Changing it means redeploying, since it is fixed at construction.
  • requireAPass = false — PersonRegistry does not check on-chain A-Pass possession before binding a wallet. See the trust note below.

The registrar is trusted, and that is the weakest joint

Person resolution happens off-chain: a script reads customerId from Cleanverse and writes keccak256(customerId) into the registry under a registrar key. The contract cannot verify that mapping. A compromised or careless registrar could bind two wallets to one person who are not the same human, or split one human across two person IDs, and the seat count would be wrong in a way the invariant cannot detect — the invariant guarantees the count is consistent, not that it is true. Closing this properly means either an on-chain A-Pass check with a real per-wallet identity signal, or an attestation signed by Cleanverse that the contract can verify. Neither exists here.

Everything else that a production deployment would need

  • No security audit. The contracts have not been reviewed by anyone but their author.
  • Deployed from a single burner key with no multisig, timelock, or upgrade path.
  • Off-chain identity resolution is a set of manual scripts, not a monitored service with retries, alerting, or reconciliation.
  • Cleanverse's API is an unhandled single point of failure. If it is down, new people cannot be resolved. The contract keeps enforcing on already-bound state, which is the right failure mode, but nothing is degraded gracefully by design.
  • The demo identities are fictional people registered against a shared sandbox. The credentials are real API objects; the humans are not.
  • No issuer-facing interface. Rule packs are constructor arguments, not a product.
What is genuinely proven. That a contract you deploy yourself can be registered as a Cleanverse A-Token and a Validator pool simultaneously while keeping its own transfer hook; that person-level headcount can be enforced inside that hook at O(1); that wallet-splitting fails against it; and that a credential frozen through Cleanverse changes the verdict on a live wallet. Every one of those is a real transaction on Monad testnet, linked from the dashboard.

FAQ

Why not just check identity at the door, like everyone else?
Because a per-wallet identity check answers "is this person allowed," and a headcount exemption asks "would the whole holder base still be legal." Those are different questions, and the second one is the one that actually voids an offering.
What stops someone from verifying under a fake identity to get extra seats?
Nothing Quorum controls directly, that risk sits with Cleanverse's own KYC process. Quorum's guarantee is narrower and firmer: given a correct KYC hash from Cleanverse, the count of distinct hashes never exceeds the cap.
Does a frozen holder lose their shares?
No. Freezing blocks new acquisition. It does not touch an existing balance, and it does not free the seat, because the holder still legally holds the security.
Why Monad?
It's the chain Cleanverse's sandbox in this hackathon runs on, and the seat check needs to be cheap enough to run on every transfer without becoming the bottleneck.
